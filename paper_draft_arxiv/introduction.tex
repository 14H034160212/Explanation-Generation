\section{Introduction}

An educational explanation for a multiple-choice question should say \emph{why} the correct option is correct. Models fine-tuned on student-written explanations (SFT) produce fluent text that often fails to do this: in our data, LLaMA-2-13B SFT outputs run to 200--290 words, contain citation-style URLs in 44--69\% of cases, and rarely entail the correct answer (Appendix~\ref{sec:failure_mode_appendix}).

Preference optimisation such as DPO \citep{rafailov2023direct} could improve this, but it needs preference labels. Human labels are expensive, and LLM judges prefer longer answers \citep{zheng2024judging,wang2023large}; Section~\ref{sec:verbosity} shows the same pattern with GPT-4o-mini on our data. An automatic, length-neutral signal is attractive. \textbf{Natural-language inference (NLI)} offers one: an NLI model takes a premise and a hypothesis and outputs the probability that the premise \emph{entails} the hypothesis. With the explanation as premise and the correct option as hypothesis, this probability is high when the explanation supports the answer.

We call the resulting pipeline \textbf{RLearner-LLM} (``R'' for reinforcement learning, replacing the iterative refinement of our earlier ILearner-LLM, \citealp{bao2025exploring}). It samples several explanations per question, scores them with NLI, optionally combined with a verifier score, and trains with DPO on the best- versus worst-scoring pair.

The obvious risk is that an explanation can entail the answer simply by \emph{restating} it. This paper is about telling the two apart. Besides the usual comparison, we use three checks: (i) SFT and DPO scored in the \emph{same} evaluation run, since sampled decoding (used for LLaMA-2) moves scores between runs; (ii) a larger NLI scorer that plays no part in training; and (iii) an \textbf{answer-masking test}, which replaces every key term of the correct option in the explanation with \texttt{[MASK]} before scoring. An explanation that only restates the answer loses its entailment under masking; one that argues for it keeps some.

Figure~\ref{fig:masking} summarises what the checks find. Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The masking test separates restatement from support.} On LLaMA-2-13B, NLI-reward DPO raises entailment two- to five-fold in four of five datasets, but under masking every gain turns into a loss: the model learns to restate the answer in a shorter output. On Gemma-4-E4B, the held-out scorer improves in four of five datasets and the masked score in all five, while outputs get shorter and copy the answer no more often (\S\ref{sec:main_results}).
\item \textbf{The verifier adds little to NLI alone.} In our preference data the chosen explanation has a \emph{lower} verifier score than the rejected one in 4--34\% of pairs. Within the same pipeline, NLI-only DPO matches the combined reward, and verifier-only DPO produces the same LLaMA-2 gain. On a single Gemma~4 candidate pool scored by every signal, NLI alone beats every verifier mixture, and verifier-only DPO lowers entailment while doubling length (\S\ref{sec:single_dual}).
\item \textbf{Entailment and judged reasoning are different targets.} A pairwise reasoning-judge reward trained with SimPO \citep{meng2024simpo} yields explanations that three independent LLM judges and four human raters prefer to SFT at matched length. The same judges barely prefer the NLI-trained policy, although its entailment is twice as high. On the shared Gemma~4 pool (five courses) judges prefer SFT to NLI-DPO; summing the judge and NLI scores on length-matched pairs keeps a significant masked entailment gain and is preferred to NLI-DPO by all three judges, so the two signals are complementary (\S\ref{sec:single_dual}).
\end{enumerate}
